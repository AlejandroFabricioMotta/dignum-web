Let the machine propose and the person approve

November 9, 2026 · 5-minute read

Automating is not letting go of control. It is deciding clearly what the machine can do on its own and what it cannot.

The difference between a routine that helps and one that scares comes down to one question: can this be undone?

Think of a repetitive task: updating your organization's public directory every week, with data, photos, and credentials. Done by hand, it's slow and full of errors. Done without control, a mistake gets published for everyone to see.

In the Mentors Directory case, the public directory, available in three languages, is updated every week from the official database. A copy is saved before touching each file. If there are bulk changes, a person reviews a sample before continuing. And previous versions are kept.

The rule is simple: what can be undone, the routine does on its own; what can't be undone asks for approval. Correcting a data point with a prior copy is reversible. Deleting, merging, or publishing at scale is not.

The same applies to any routine: a weekly report, a mass email, a data update. If it prepares and proposes, we take advantage of its speed. If it also decides on its own when something has no way back, no one can answer for what happened.

With this rule, the routine saves hours and the team stops living in fear that something will break. Before scheduling any automatic task, it's worth answering three questions:

  1. What is the worst that can happen if it makes a mistake?
  2. Is there a copy to go back to?
  3. Who, by name, approves what can't be undone?

Put it into practice: Which task in your organization would you automate today if you knew nothing irreversible happens without a person approving it?
