Recommending is not deciding for someone else

November 9, 2026 · 5-minute read

A good recommendation doesn't push. It offers a path and lets each person choose their own.

Systems that suggest what to watch or buy can help a lot, or trap people. The difference lies in how they are designed.

You enter a platform with a huge amount of material. Without a guide, you get lost. And if you leave without finding anything, neither you nor the platform gained anything. That's what recommending is for.

It works more simply than it seems. The content is tagged (what it's about, who it's for) and the people are tagged (what they usually watch, what interests them). Then both profiles are cross-matched and the browsing screen shows the best match first. That's how it was done in the RGM+ case, a multimedia platform.

In practice, the platform doesn't need to know everything about you: it's enough to understand which materials are useful to you. And the team can explain, in simple words, why each suggestion appears first.

The risk appears when the recommendation becomes a mandate. If you only see what you already like, you never discover anything new. And if the criterion is a black box, the team can't correct it when it's wrong.

That's why, for Dignum, wisdom is guided, not dictated. A well-designed recommendation has three safeguards:

  1. Clear, written criteria that the team can read and change.
  2. Always an open path to the whole catalog, so each person can choose something else.
  3. A person who reviews what is recommended and why.

With these safeguards, technology organizes abundance and the decision remains with the one who chooses.

Put it into practice: Do your customers choose what they see, or see what someone chose for them?
