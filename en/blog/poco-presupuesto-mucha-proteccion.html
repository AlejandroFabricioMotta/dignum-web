Small budget, strong protection: what you can do when your business stops being local

November 10, 2026 · 5-minute read

When you sell in your neighborhood, data is a notebook. When you sell to the world, it is the company's most valuable asset. And protecting it doesn't take a huge budget: it takes order.

Many small companies think security is a luxury for big ones. It is understandable: the budget is tight and there are a thousand urgencies. But the moment a business goes from local to global is exactly when it accumulates the most data (customers in other countries, international payments, contracts, its own developments) and when it becomes most interesting to anyone who wants to steal it.

The right question is not “how much do I need to spend?” but “what protects most of the risk at the lowest cost?”

What doing nothing costs

IBM's 2025 report calculated that a data breach costs, on a global average, 4.44 million dollars. It is an average across organizations of every kind and size, so it is not the figure for a small company. But it helps size the order of magnitude against what prevention costs.

The same study also found that 63% of organizations had no policies to manage artificial intelligence or detect its unauthorized use. That matters a lot today: breaches with high “shadow AI” use cost 670,000 dollars more. A clear rule about which AI tools can be used and with what data costs almost nothing.

Five low-cost decisions that cover the essentials

The US cybersecurity agency (CISA) proposes four basic practices for any business: train staff against phishing, require strong passwords, require two-step verification and keep software up to date. To these we add a fifth that most experts recommend: having backups.

1. A password manager for the whole team. Business plans are usually billed as a monthly subscription per person (ask for a quote), and they end the habit of repeating passwords.

2. Two-step verification, starting with email, the bank and the cloud. Microsoft estimates that the phishing-resistant version blocks more than 99% of this type of attack, even if the attacker has the right password. Many services include it at no extra cost.

3. Automatic updates. It is a checkbox you turn on once.

4. Tested backups. The 3-2-1 rule (three copies, two media, one outside your office) can be met with an external drive and a cloud service.

5. Short, repeated training. A 20-minute talk every quarter on how to spot a fake email pays off more than any expensive tool. CISA notes that phishing is the most common way data gets compromised.

Prices depend on each provider and country; it is worth asking for a quote before deciding.

Get organized before you buy

Before spending, make a one-page inventory: what data the company holds, where it is, who accesses it and what would happen if it were lost. That already tells you where to invest first and what can wait.

It is also worth knowing from when your data crosses borders. If you start selling to or working with people in other countries, data protection rules different from your own country's may apply to you. That is a good moment to consult a specialized lawyer.

And one idea that matters to us: security is not only technology, it is human judgment. An informed team and one responsible person are worth more than any expensive equipment with no owner.

Put it into practice:

  1. Make the one-page inventory: your five most valuable pieces of data, where they live and who accesses them.
  2. Choose a password manager and turn it on for the whole team this month.
  3. Turn on two-step verification on email, bank and cloud storage.
  4. Set a date for the first restore test of your backups.
  5. Write a half-page rule on the use of AI tools and which data must not be given to them.

Sources: IBM, Cost of a Data Breach Report 2025 (July 30, 2025); Microsoft, “Extortion and ransomware drive over half of cyberattacks” (Digital Defense Report 2025, October 2025); CISA, “Four Cybersecurity Essentials for Businesses.”
