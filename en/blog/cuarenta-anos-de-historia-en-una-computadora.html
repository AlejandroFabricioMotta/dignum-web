Forty years of your company's history fit on a single computer. Where is the backup?

November 10, 2026 · 5-minute read

Those who built the company through sacrifice rarely imagine that all that road can be erased in one afternoon. It is not carelessness: nobody explained the risk to them in their own language.

Traditional senior leaders built businesses when information lived in folders, ledgers and people's memory. Today that same information (customers, prices, contracts, formulas, years of invoices) lives on a computer, in an email account or in a cloud service that someone set up a long time ago and nobody has looked at since.

It is not a lack of intelligence. It is that nobody took the time to show them, with simple examples, what can be lost and what can be done.

The risks you can't see

The senior leader usually thinks about money being stolen. But what hurts most, in practice, is what cannot be replaced:

  • The computer that breaks or gets lost, with the only copy of years of work.
  • The email account someone hijacks, from which your customers are asked for money in your name.
  • Data hijacking (ransomware): a program encrypts everything and demands payment to give it back.
  • The key person who leaves, taking the passwords, the access and the knowledge of where everything is.

The third point deserves a number. Verizon's 2025 report found that ransomware was present in 88% of the breaches affecting small and medium-sized businesses, compared with 39% in large ones. Attackers know a small company usually has fewer defenses and can't afford to stop.

And the fourth is the most human. When a person with decades of craft retires, it is not just an employee who leaves: it is knowledge. It is the same concern that led us to create Dignum, and it applies to data and access too.

What can be done, without being technical

The good news is that the most important steps don't require understanding technology. They require deciding and assigning someone.

1. A copy that doesn't depend on the computer. The most widespread rule is called 3-2-1: three copies of what matters, on two types of media, with one kept outside your office. The US cybersecurity agency (CISA) recommends it, and its ransomware guide warns that many variants look for and encrypt or delete accessible backups, so at least one copy should be kept offline.

2. Test that the copy works. A backup that has never been restored is a hope, not a backup. Once a year, have someone recover any file in front of you.

3. A second key on email and banking. Two-step verification is the cheapest and most effective defense there is.

4. Know who has which access. A simple list: who gets into email, the bank, invoicing and the cloud. If someone leaves, their access is removed that same day.

5. A transition plan for knowledge. Access details, suppliers and key procedures must be written down and kept somewhere safe, not only in one person's head. Here security and legacy meet.

The leader doesn't need to know code. They need to ask.

Three questions are enough to start: Where is the backup of what I can't lose? When did we last test it? Who else besides me knows how to recover it? If the answer is silence, you already know where to begin.

Put it into practice:

  1. This week, identify the five files or systems without which your company can't open on Monday.
  2. Check that each one has a copy outside your office, and have it restored as a test.
  3. Turn on two-step verification on company email and on the bank.
  4. Name one person responsible for access, with a backup.
  5. Write down on paper (kept under lock) the critical access details and who can recover them.

Sources: Verizon, 2025 Data Breach Investigations Report (April 23, 2025); CISA, #StopRansomware Guide and 3-2-1 backup guidance; CISA, “Four Cybersecurity Essentials for Businesses.”
