Before you put an AI agent in a role, answer one question: what will that person do tomorrow?

November 10, 2026 · 5-minute read

Automating looks like a technical decision until the day someone loses their place. From that moment on, it is a leadership decision.

Monday, management meeting. Someone shares their screen and proudly shows that the new AI agent already reconciles the month's invoices in minutes. Polite applause, a couple of questions about the license cost, and on to the next item on the agenda.

Nobody asks about Marta, who has been reconciling those invoices for eleven years. Nobody asks what Marta knows that the agent doesn't yet, or what she will be doing on Tuesday.

That unasked question is the subject of this post.

AI is already coming in, with or without a plan

An AI agent can answer inquiries, draft proposals, organize an inventory and work twenty-four hours a day without getting tired. It is no longer a promise: in many companies it has already arrived, sometimes through the front door and sometimes because someone tried it on their own and it worked.

What almost never arrives with the tool is a plan for the people. And that is where the problem starts, because technology decides nothing: it does what it is asked to do. The decisions are still ours, and they are decisions we will later have to answer for.

Three places from which decisions are made

Not everyone has the same lever, but everyone has one.

The manager who redesigns the org chart decides which roles an agent takes on and which roles become more human. They can do it together with the people affected or over their heads.

The owner decides what happens with the savings that automation generates: whether everything goes to the bottom line or part of it is invested in the people who made it possible to get here.

The investor decides what to ask for. If they demand a return plan to approve an AI project, they can demand a plan for the people with the same firmness. An investor with a conscience doesn't ask only how much is saved, but how.

What a transition plan is (and what it isn't)

It is not an HR email or a one-afternoon course. It is the concrete answer, before installing the agent, to this question: what will happen to each person whose work changes? And it has three possible paths:

  • Redeployment: the person moves to another role in the company, where their judgment and knowledge of the business are worth more than ever.
  • Retraining: they learn a new function, for example supervising the agent that was going to replace them.
  • Upskilling: they receive the training needed to grow within an org chart that is now mixed, made up of people and agents.

With deadlines, with names and with an honest conversation. If you can't answer what that person will do from now on, the company is not yet ready to automate that role. It is a simple rule and, precisely for that reason, an uncomfortable one.

It's not only a moral question: it's also a business one

The numbers show that the transition is enormous. The World Economic Forum projects that through 2030, 170 million jobs will be created and 92 million will be displaced, a net gain of 78 million. But it also estimates that around 40% of the skills required in jobs will change, and that 41% of companies worldwide plan to reduce staff where AI can automate. This is not a story of work disappearing: it is a story of transition. And every poorly supported transition leaves people behind.

Inside the company, the cost shows up sooner than you'd think. When a person suspects that a machine will take their place and nobody explains anything to them, they stop sharing what they know. They protect themselves. And an organization where people protect themselves begins to go dark from within.

There are lessons from outside, too. The Swedish payments company Klarna announced in 2024 that its AI assistant was doing the work of 700 customer service agents, and its headcount fell by 22%. A year later, its CEO acknowledged that quality had dropped and the company went back to hiring people. AI saved costs, but it lost something the customer noticed. Someone should have designed what gets automated and what doesn't.

AI didn't come to displace: it came to empower

The conclusion is not “don't automate.” It is to automate with judgment. At Dignum we believe that artificial intelligence and robotics did not arrive to displace people, but to empower them and to let companies scale to a reach that once seemed reserved for the big ones. But that promise is only fulfilled if someone takes care of designing it.

And we have to be honest about something more: even with the best plan, not every case will find a way out inside the same company. We talk about that, and about who takes responsibility, in another post.

Put it into practice: pick a repetitive task in your company. Imagine an agent does it tomorrow. What will the person who does it today do? If the answer doesn't exist yet, that is your task for this week.

Sources: World Economic Forum, Future of Jobs Report 2025 (January 2025); Entrepreneur, “Klarna Is Hiring Customer Service Agents After AI Couldn't Cut It” (May 2025).
