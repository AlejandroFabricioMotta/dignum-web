Who pays for the transition? An uncomfortable proposal for when redeployment isn't enough

November 10, 2026 · 5-minute read

We can redeploy, retrain and upskill. And still, someone will probably be left out. What we do with that part is the hardest question to ask.

We are going to say something that isn't always said in the business world: redeploying and retraining will not be enough for everyone.

We say it without giving up. At Dignum we hold that the first effort must always be to redeploy, retrain and upskill. But one of our principles is transparency and truth, and that obliges us to look squarely at what remains afterward.

The new equilibrium we imagine

Our hypothesis is this. If companies can do more with the same resources, they don't shrink: they scale. More companies are born, because starting a business costs less. Existing ones reach farther, into territories they couldn't serve before, and sell more. But with more companies and more supply, competition tightens: profit margins fall, production costs fall and prices follow. And that continues until a new global equilibrium is reached.

We stress that this is a working hypothesis, not a certainty. History shows transitions that ended up creating more work than they destroyed, and the World Economic Forum's projections for 2030 point in that direction, with a net gain of 78 million jobs. But a net gain in the total doesn't give a job to a specific 54-year-old in a city where the position they held no longer exists. The equilibrium arrives on a global scale; the cost of getting there is paid by specific people, in specific timeframes.

The remainder: people left without a place

Let's imagine a company that did everything right: it designed a transition plan and offered redeployment, retraining and upskilling. Even so, some people will not manage to fit into the new labor landscape. Because of age, because of where they live, because of the kind of trade they practice, because of the pace at which the market changes. Not for lack of willingness or worth.

We call that group the remainder. And the question is who takes responsibility for them.

Our proposal: let AI help sustain the transition

We put forward an idea for debate: that this remainder be supported by a subsidy funded by a global tax on the use of artificial intelligence in production processes.

The logic is simple. Automation generates a productivity gain that concentrates in those who use it. It seems reasonable that part of that gain should fund the transition of those left out while the world builds its new equilibrium. Not as a punishment for innovation, but as a way for its benefit to be shared.

The idea of taxing automation is not new: it has been debated for years, with defenders and serious critics. And it's worth listening to the critics.

The objections we have to take seriously

  • How do you measure “AI use”? Defining what is taxed and how, without it becoming impractical or arbitrary, is a real technical problem.
  • What if it slows down innovation? A poorly designed tax can discourage precisely the adoption that SMEs need. In Latin America, micro, small and medium-sized enterprises are 99.5% of companies and generate around 61% of employment, yet contribute only 25% of GDP (ECLAC, 2019). AI can narrow that gap or widen it, and a levy that hits small companies harder than large ones would work against it. Any design should protect SMEs.
  • What if every country does it differently? If the tax is local, companies can move to wherever less is paid. That's why we speak of a global agreement, and we acknowledge that it is the hardest part politically.
  • Doesn't it become an excuse? There is a risk that “after all, there's a subsidy” replaces the effort to redeploy. For us, the subsidy is a safety net, not plan A. And income alone doesn't give back what work provides: belonging and purpose. It has to come with opportunities for training and participation.

We don't have all the answers, and we don't claim to. These questions are precisely the reason for opening the conversation.

What you can do without waiting for any global agreement

While the debate goes on, every organization can take a step: create its own transition fund. Set aside a percentage of the savings each AI agent generates for the training, redeployment and support of the people affected. It is a company-scale version of the same idea, with one huge advantage: it depends on a decision, not on an international treaty.

That is the meaning of the principle that commits us most: impact before profitability. If we have to choose, we choose impact.

Put it into practice: if your company saved part of its costs today thanks to an AI agent, what percentage of those savings would you set aside for the people whose work changed? Put a number on it and write it down. It is the first draft of your own transition fund.
