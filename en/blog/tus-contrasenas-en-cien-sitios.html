You left your password on a hundred sites. Do you know which country each one lives in?

November 10, 2026 · 5-minute read

Your identity is no longer in your wallet or your drawer. It is spread across hundreds of digital companies you never chose carefully.

Think about what you did this week. You paid a bill from your banking app, signed up on a platform to watch a course, visited an online store, accepted some terms without reading them. At every step you left an email, a password, sometimes a document or a card number.

Now ask yourself an uncomfortable question: where is that company registered? What law obliges it to protect what you gave it? What happens to your data if it is sold, goes bankrupt or suffers an attack?

Banking identity went digital. Our habits didn't.

Twenty years ago, your financial identity was a teller window and a signature. Today it is an app, a password and a code that arrives on your phone. That is progress, and also concentration: whoever gets your access gets your money, your history and, often, your company's.

The data shows it. According to Microsoft, more than 97% of identity attacks are password attacks, and in the first half of 2025 identity-based attacks grew by 32%. Verizon's 2025 breach report agrees on the essentials: credential abuse was the most common way in, at 22% of the cases analyzed.

You don't need a genius to get in. A reused password that already appeared in a leak is enough.

The underlying problem: you don't know who you are dealing with

When you hand data to a platform, you also hand over a jurisdiction. The company may be registered in a country with strict data protection laws or in one where they barely exist. It may store your information on servers in a third country, under different rules.

Some rules travel beyond their borders. The European data protection regulation, for example, also reaches companies outside the Union that offer services to people living there. Other countries have narrower laws. Argentina has Law 25,326, and the European Commission confirmed in 2024 that its level of protection remains adequate for transfers of data from Europe. It is a good example that rules exist but change from place to place, and almost nobody checks them before clicking “Accept.”

For a person, the risk is fraud and impersonation. For a company, it is worse: employees use those same platforms with their corporate email, and a leaked personal account can open the door to the company's account.

What a company loses when it loses control of its access

  • Money: fraudulent transfers and diverted payments.
  • Trust: customers who stop believing their data is safe.
  • Continuity: if email or the bank is locked, the business stalls.
  • Liability: in many countries, losing third-party personal data has legal consequences for whoever holds it.

At Dignum we call it security and confidentiality by design: thinking about protection before something fails, not after. And always with a person making the decisions, because technology alone doesn't know what matters to you.

Put it into practice:

  1. Use a password manager and stop repeating passwords. It is the first step recommended by the US cybersecurity agency (CISA).
  2. Turn on two-step verification on email and banking first, before anywhere else. Microsoft estimates it blocks more than 99% of identity attacks (in its phishing-resistant form).
  3. Before signing up for a new platform, look up which country it operates in and what its privacy policy says about where it stores data.
  4. List the ten accounts you cannot afford to lose and check who has access to each one.

Sources: Microsoft, “Extortion and ransomware drive over half of cyberattacks” (Digital Defense Report 2025, October 2025); Verizon, 2025 Data Breach Investigations Report (April 23, 2025); CISA, “Four Cybersecurity Essentials for Businesses”; Legiscope, “Does GDPR Apply to Companies Outside of the European Union?”; Marval O'Farrell Mairal, “The European Commission confirms that Argentina has adequate rules…” (February 23, 2024).
