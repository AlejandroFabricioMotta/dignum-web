Teach the business to the young, teach AI to the veteran: the cross-training almost nobody is doing

November 10, 2026 · 5-minute read

Those who arrived yesterday and those who have been here for thirty years share the same fear and know different things. Together they make the best possible mixed org chart.

In almost every company there are two people who don't talk to each other as much as they should.

One is the young person who joined a few months ago. Let's call them a Young Technology Native: they open a new tool and understand it in twenty minutes, they ask an AI agent what would take others an afternoon, and they're not afraid to try. But they still don't know why the company's processes are the way they are. They don't know the customer who has to be called first, or the mistake made ten years ago that nobody wants to repeat.

The other is the Traditional Professional: decades in the company. They know the why behind every decision and the exceptions that appear in no manual. And they look with distance, sometimes with wariness, at the tools the other uses so naturally.

We should clarify that this is a portrait, not a rule: there are young people who understand the business better than anyone, and veterans who jump into using AI before everyone else. But the pattern is frequent enough to design around it.

Each one holds half of what the company needs. And most organizations, when they bring in AI, keep them apart: they train the young person in tools and the veteran in nothing, or simply write them off.

First half: help the young person understand how the process works

A well-designed AI agent isn't built on a process nobody understands. If the young person is going to work with agents (configuring, supervising and improving them), they need to know the business from end to end: where an order comes from, who approves it, where it gets stuck, what happens when something goes wrong.

That understanding isn't learned from a presentation. It is learned by sitting next to someone who has done it for years, mapping the process together and asking why. The young person stops being “the one who knows technology” and becomes someone able to translate the company's knowledge into instructions an agent can follow.

Second half: help the traditional professional learn to interact with agents

The veteran doesn't need to learn to program. They need to learn to direct: how to ask an agent for things, how to judge whether the result is good, how to correct it and when not to trust it. In a mixed org chart, above every agent there is a person with a first and last name who supervises it and answers for what it does. Who better for that role than someone who knows the business inside and out?

It is a retraining that respects a career instead of erasing it. The message changes completely: no longer “a machine will do your job,” but “your judgment is what this machine needs in order to work well.”

The bridge: so knowledge doesn't retire with the people

Here something appears that we consider central. While we debate AI, the people who built our organizations are retiring, taking decades of judgment with them. An APQC study from October 2025, focused on the United States and covering a thousand professionals, found that 92% of organizations do not consistently capture the knowledge of those about to retire. It is data from another market, but anyone who has seen someone with thirty years in the company walk out the door recognizes the phenomenon.

Cross-training is also the way to rescue that knowledge. When the young person and the veteran work together on the process, what the latter knows and was never written down begins to be written. With that material, a company can build what we call a wisdom agent: an agent made to measure, which knows its regulations, its history and its hard data, and which incorporates the reflected experience of those who lived it.

Two principles guide us here. Wisdom is guided, not dictated: the agent accompanies decisions, it doesn't impose them. And experience belongs to those who lived it and to those who hire it: nobody appropriates another person's knowledge without their consent. That is why it has to be asked for with respect, and by explaining what it's for.

Why this is an opportunity and not a threat

The regional data helps show the scale. The ILO and the World Bank estimate that between 26% and 38% of jobs in Latin America are exposed to generative AI. But most of that exposure is complementarity, not replacement: between 2% and 5% could be fully automated, while between 8% and 14% could improve their productivity if properly supported.

And a 2025 MIT study found that 95% of generative AI pilots in companies fail to deliver measurable impact, and that those that partner with specialists and integrate the tool into their real way of working succeed about two times out of three. Technology doesn't fail for lack of power, but for lack of human design.

Cross-training is human design in its purest form. With it, the arrival of artificial intelligence and robotics displaces no one without giving them tools: it empowers both and lets the company grow without losing what makes it unique.

Put it into practice: think of a young person and a veteran in your company. What could each teach the other? If the answer makes you want to sit them down together on Monday, you already have the first step of your training plan.

Sources: ILO and World Bank, via EFE/El Día (July 31, 2024); Fortune, MIT report on generative AI pilots (August 18, 2025); APQC, via Business Wire, The Great Retirement (October 13, 2025).
